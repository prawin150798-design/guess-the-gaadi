Stories

A few longer looks at the cars behind the clues.

The Ambassador's 50-year run

Few cars anywhere in the world stayed in production as long, largely unchanged, as the Hindustan Ambassador. Built from 1958 to 2014, its rounded body traced back to the British Morris Oxford III, yet it became something distinctly Indian: the default car for government convoys, the workhorse of Kolkata's taxi ranks, and for decades the only "proper car" many Indian families ever rode in. Its slow fade wasn't really about the car failing — it was about the market around it changing faster than the Ambassador ever needed to.

How the Maruti 800 changed what "owning a car" meant

Before 1983, a car in India was often something you waited years for, with a long waitlist attached to just a handful of models. The Maruti 800 broke that pattern. Small, simple, and radically cheaper to run than anything else on sale, it turned car ownership from an aspiration into something achievable for an emerging middle class. Its three-cylinder, 796cc engine wasn't fast by any measure — but the 800 wasn't selling speed, it was selling access.

The Sierra's Alpine window, twice over

Tata's Sierra was an odd, interesting car when it launched in 1991: a three-door SUV with a curved rear glass panel sweeping up into the roofline, nicknamed the "Alpine window." It never sold in huge numbers, but it stuck in memory precisely because nothing else on Indian roads looked quite like it. Decades later, Tata brought the name back for 2025 with a new Sierra that deliberately echoes that glasshouse silhouette — a rare case of a design detail being distinctive enough to revive on its own.

Why the Padmini became Mumbai's taxi, and almost nothing else

The Premier Padmini, based on the Fiat 1100 Delight, is remembered today almost entirely for one thing: its black-and-yellow life as Mumbai's "kaali-peeli" taxi. That reputation somewhat obscures that it was, for a while, a genuine private-car rival to the Ambassador. But as Fiat's involvement faded and newer hatchbacks arrived in the 1980s, the Padmini's second life as a taxi fleet mainstay outlasted its life as a family car — until the last ones were finally retired from Mumbai's streets in 2019.

From the Gypsy to the Jimny: a 4x4 badge inherited twice

Maruti's light off-roader has effectively been reinvented once already. The original Gypsy, based on the Suzuki SJ410, became a fixture with the Indian Army, police units, and hill-station drivers who needed something light and genuinely capable rather than merely SUV-shaped. When Maruti Suzuki brought the five-door Jimny to India in 2023, it wasn't a direct replacement on paper — but it inherited the same basic promise: a small, boxy, ladder-frame 4x4 built for people who actually leave the tarmac.
